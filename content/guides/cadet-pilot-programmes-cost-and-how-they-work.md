Airline cadet programmes take people with no flying experience and put them through everything they need to sit in the right-hand seat of an airliner. The best-known schemes - British Airways' Speedbird Pilot Academy, Lufthansa's European Flight Academy, Qantas Future Pilot Program, Qatar Airways Cadet Pilot - share the same shape, but they differ a lot on who pays. This guide covers the three main types, what they really cost, and the warning signs that separate a legitimate programme from a scam.

Costs and openings change. Always check the airline's own careers page before you commit anything.

## The three types of programme

| Type | Who pays | Job at the end | Common examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Fully sponsored** | Airline pays training; cadet gets a modest allowance | Yes, conditional on passing each stage | Rare. Historically Cathay Pacific, some national carriers; most are closed or paused |
| **Partially sponsored / loan-backed** | Cadet pays (often through an airline-arranged loan), repaid from future salary | Yes, if the airline is still hiring at completion | British Airways Speedbird, Qantas Future Pilot (various intakes) |
| **Self-funded ab initio** | Cadet pays in full | Interview opportunity, not guaranteed job | Lufthansa European Flight Academy (now mostly self-funded), CTC Wings with partner airlines, most Indian airline cadet schemes |

The important point: **most "airline cadet" programmes today are self-funded or loan-backed, not free**. The airline screens you, puts you through a managed course, and offers an interview or a conditional job offer at the end. You still pay for the training.

## What a cadet programme actually covers

A full ab initio cadet programme takes 18–24 months and covers everything from the PPL and ATPL ground theory, through CPL and instrument rating, to Multi-Crew Cooperation (MCC) and a type rating on the airline's aircraft (A320, 737, A350 or similar). A graduate holds a frozen ATPL (CPL + instrument rating + ATPL theory) with a specific type rating. They then join the airline as a cadet First Officer and complete line training on real revenue flights.

## What it costs

Programme fees are set by each airline and training organisation, not by any regulator, and they vary widely by school, country, aircraft type and whether a type rating is bundled. There is no official published figure.

**Before you commit money**, ask the airline or the training organisation that runs the programme for:

- A written fee schedule covering every stage (ground theory, PPL, hour-building, CPL, instrument rating, MCC/JOC, type rating)
- Which costs are included (books, exams, uniform, medical) and which are extra
- Living costs - the course usually happens away from home, in a country where you cannot earn
- The payment schedule: instalments tied to training stages are safer than a lump sum up front
- Whether the fee includes a type rating on the airline's aircraft, and which fleet
- The repayment terms if the scheme is loan-backed

**Compare carefully.** A cheaper school that takes an extra 12 months to finish is not cheaper once you count lost earnings and living costs.

## Sponsored vs self-funded: what to look at

Beyond the headline fee, these questions tell you whether a programme is worth it:

- **Is there a conditional job offer in writing before you start?** A real sponsored or partially-sponsored programme gives you one. A self-funded course does not.
- **What happens if you fail a stage?** Some programmes exit cadets who fail twice. Others let you repeat at your own cost. Read the terms.
- **Who holds the money?** The airline, the training school, or an escrow account? Instalments tied to training stages are safer than a lump sum paid up front.
- **How many previous graduates got the airline job?** Ask for numbers from the last three intakes, not stories.
- **Which fleet is the type rating on?** A type rating with no job attached is less useful - airlines usually prefer to train their own.
- **Does the airline still exist and still hire?** Loan-backed cadet schemes depend on the airline being there and recruiting when you finish. Pandemic-era cadets found this out the hard way.

## How you get in

Selection for a cadet scheme is tougher than for a First Officer job once you already have a licence. Airlines are picking people they will train and employ for 30 years. Steps typically include:

1. **Online application** - CV, academic record, motivation statement
2. **Online tests** - numerical, verbal and spatial reasoning; pilot-specific coordination and multi-tasking tests (DLR, COMPASS, ADAPT, PILAPT variants)
3. **Video interview** - recorded answers to competency questions
4. **Assessment day** - group exercises, interview, simulator exercise (handling an aircraft you have never flown)
5. **Medical and background checks**

Pass rates are not published by most airlines, but the schemes are competitive by design: airlines run them because they want to hand-pick a small group they will employ for decades. Preparation matters - practice tests and simulator preparation at a specialist centre have become part of the normal path.

## Who they look for

Common requirements across the big programmes:

- Minimum age 17 or 18 at application, usually under 30 at joining
- Secondary school completed with mathematics and physics (or clear equivalent)
- Fluent English - ICAO level 4 at the start, better by the end
- Class 1 medical certificate - the aviation authority's medical, not a GP letter
- No criminal record, no outstanding visa issues
- Legal right to live in the airline's base country (sometimes relaxed if the airline sponsors a visa)

A prior degree is not usually required, but is sometimes preferred. Flying hours before the programme are not required - some schemes actively prefer applicants with none, so they can shape habits from scratch.

## Warning signs of a scam

Cadet schemes are a favourite target for fake "aviation agents" because the fees are high and candidates are eager. Walk away if:

- An agent asks for a fee to submit your application. Airlines accept applications directly - always free.
- A "guaranteed job" is promised in exchange for a payment. No legitimate scheme can promise this; even sponsored cadets must pass each stage.
- The offer letter comes from a free email address (gmail.com, yahoo.com) or a domain that is not the airline's own.
- You are asked to pay a "visa fee", "medical fee" or "uniform fee" before you have joined.
- Payment is requested in cryptocurrency or to a personal bank account.

## If you can't afford a cadet programme

A cadet programme is not the only way to the right-hand seat. Many pilots fund their own PPL and CPL at a flying school, build hours as an instructor or in general aviation, and apply as "modular" candidates to airlines. The total cost is similar or lower; the route is slower (three to five years before the first airline job) and less managed, but it leaves you with a licence you can take anywhere.

## Where to look for openings

Cadet programmes open and close with little notice. The best approach is to pick two or three airlines whose scheme interests you and watch their careers pages.
