Airline jobs attract scammers for a simple reason: they are jobs people dream about. A cabin crew role in the Gulf, a first officer seat with a big carrier, a well-paid engineering contract abroad. Fraudsters know that excitement can push careful people into rushing. This guide covers the warning signs, how to check an offer, and what to do if you think you have been targeted.

## The golden rule: real employers do not ask you to pay

Genuine airlines, airports and aerospace companies do not charge candidates to apply, to attend an interview, or to receive an offer. They also do not ask new hires to send money for a visa, a work permit, a medical test, uniforms, accommodation deposits or "processing fees" before they start.

There are legitimate costs in some aviation careers. Pilots pay for flight training and, in some cases, for type ratings. Engineers pay for licence exams. But those payments go to approved training organisations or aviation authorities, and you choose to make them. They are never a hidden condition of a job offer that arrives out of the blue.

If any step in the process involves you sending money to a person or company to secure the job, treat it as a scam until proven otherwise.

## Warning signs to look for

**An offer you did not apply for.** You receive a message saying you have been selected for a role, but you never applied, or you applied months ago through a different site.

**No real interview.** Real airline recruitment involves assessment days, group exercises, simulator checks, technical interviews or video interviews with named people. A scam often skips straight from "we liked your CV" to "here is your contract".

**Free email addresses.** Messages come from Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo or similar addresses rather than the airline's own domain. Be careful with look-alike domains too: an extra letter, a hyphen, or a different ending such as ".org" or ".net" instead of the company's real address.

**Pressure and urgency.** You are told the offer expires in 24 hours, that there are only two seats left in the training batch, or that you must pay today to hold your place.

**Requests for sensitive documents too early.** Asking for a passport copy, bank details or national ID before any genuine interview is a red flag. Real employers ask for these later in the process, through secure systems.

**Salary that seems too good.** Unusually high pay, tax-free packages and generous benefits for an entry-level role are bait. Compare the offer with similar roles listed on the employer's own careers site.

**Poor spelling and odd formatting.** Large employers have recruitment teams and legal departments. Contracts full of errors, blurry logos or mismatched fonts suggest a forgery.

**Payment to a third party.** The message names a "travel agency", "visa consultant" or "immigration lawyer" who you must pay. The airline itself never appears in the money trail.

**Contact through messaging apps only.** The whole process runs over WhatsApp or Telegram, with no official email, website login or phone number you can verify.

## How to check whether a job is real

1. **Find the job on the employer's own careers site.** Go to the airline's official website yourself, by typing its address or searching for it, rather than clicking a link in the message. If the role is not listed there, be very cautious. Every job on this site links to the employer's own careers page for exactly this reason.
2. **Check the sender's email domain.** It should match the company's official website domain exactly.
3. **Contact the employer independently.** Use the contact details on the official website, not those in the message, and ask whether the offer is genuine. Many airlines publish a fraud warning page and an address for reporting fake recruiters.
4. **Search the recruiter's name and the wording of the message.** Scam messages are often reused. Searching a distinctive sentence can reveal reports from other people.
5. **Ask for a video interview with a named recruiter.** Then check that person exists at the company. Scammers usually avoid this.
6. **Never pay to "secure" a role.** If you are asked to pay, stop.

## Special care for cabin crew and cadet pilot programmes

Cabin crew recruitment is a favourite target, because airlines really do run open days in many countries. Scammers copy the format: a fake "open day" announcement, followed by a request to pay a registration or "grooming assessment" fee. Real open days are announced on the airline's own careers site and social media accounts, and attending is free.

Cadet pilot schemes are another target. Some genuine schemes are self-funded, which makes it harder to spot a fake one. Check that the programme appears on the airline's official site, that the training organisation is approved by the national aviation authority, and that any payment goes to that approved organisation under a written agreement you have read carefully.

## If you think you have been targeted

- **Stop contact and do not send money or documents.**
- **Report it to the airline or company being impersonated.** Most have a dedicated address for recruitment fraud.
- **Report it to your national police or fraud reporting service**, especially if you have already paid.
- **Contact your bank immediately** if you shared card or account details.
- **Tell us** through the contact page if a scam is using an employer listed on this site, so we can warn other readers.

## The bottom line

A real aviation job begins on a real employer's website and costs you nothing to apply for. When in doubt, go to the source. You can [browse current aviation jobs]({{ link('') }}#jobs) here, each linked directly to the employer's official careers page.
